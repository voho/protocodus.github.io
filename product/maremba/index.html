The instruments

Four instruments, built from their physics.

Each instrument family has its own geometry, material losses, supports and resonators. Those differences shape the attack, the overtones and the ring, so changing instruments changes how the sound is made.

  • Imperial Rosewood, a concert marimba

    Per-key carved rosewood bars model concert overtone tuning and frequency-dependent wood losses. Energy passes into tuned brass-tube resonators, letting the low note bloom after the attack while the upper partials ring.

  • Mayan Padauk, a Guatemalan marimba

    Hormigo-style bars are tuned at their fundamental over tapered cedar-box resonators, giving a shorter, woodier voice. Raise Buzz for pressure-driven charleo membranes, with their lower rasp reaching into the treble melody.

  • Balafon Ancestral

    Fire-cured key models follow a length progression with their own overtone ratios. Slightly offset calabash-gourd resonators and stretching mirliton membranes give firm strokes a harmonic buzz that fades with the note.

  • Kalimba Artisan

    Seventeen steel-tine positions share a yielding bridge and one resonant box. Each tine drives the box through its signed bridge force, so partials near a mounting node transmit less energy into the board. A modelled thumb presses, releases and damps each tine, producing a brief upper-partial tink over the ring. Flesh, thumb, nail, pick and tap offer five ways to play it.

  • Five strikers

    Yarn, cord, rubber, wood or the mallet’s shaft. Hardness, Position and Decay shape each stroke. Natural contact variation is always present; Variance and Artifacts add more of the player and the instrument.

  • Resonance and character

    Shape the carving, bar-to-air coupling, frame or box bloom and sympathetic keys. Membranes stretch and lose energy as they buzz; Kalimba board buzzers lift and rattle under stronger plucks. Tension also changes support damping with Buzz off.

From contact to capture

The sound follows the instrument.

Strike position, force and the vibration already in the instrument all matter. Then three distinct capture models let you hear the same performance from the air, the room or the instrument’s surface.

  • A mallet meets a moving bar

    A finite-element model supplies each key’s bending modes and shapes; off-centre strokes also excite twisting modes. The mallet head’s mass and elasticity govern its nonlinear contact, so harder strokes change the attack and a restrike interacts with the note still ringing.

  • Air and structure answer back

    Tubes, boxes and gourds exchange energy with their bars. Shared frames and the Kalimba’s bridge carry energy into unplayed keys, building a sympathetic halo as those keys resonate at the instrument’s pitches.

  • A close listening position

    The Close path projects the bars’ vibration toward two ear positions, then applies measured head-and-ear response filters. Spatial cancellation, arrival time and distance shape the stereo sound.

  • Rooms with dimensions and materials

    Wood, Chamber and Hall use defined room sizes, source-to-microphone travel and six first wall reflections. Wall absorption shapes their tone and decay, modelled condenser capsules capture the result, and a diffuse-tail model adds a gently evolving sense of space.

  • A contact pickup with a circuit

    Piezo reads surface strain through a modelled PVDF film and charge amplifier, including electrical loading and overload recovery. On Kalimba it senses the shared board driven by the tines, giving a different attack and overtone balance from the microphones.

  • Playing the instrument’s mechanics

    Move across a bar’s vibration nodes to change its overtones, use dead strokes to leave the mallet against it, or cover the Kalimba’s sound hole to shift the box resonance. The gesture changes the model that makes the note.

Playing

Rolls, damping and dead strokes are part of the model.

A repeated note meets a bar that is still moving. Mallet gestures, damping hands and the mod wheel change contact, damping and resonance inside the model.

  • Rolls and covered sound holes

    On the wooden instruments, raise the mod wheel to roll held keys with alternating mallets, from six to twenty strokes a second. On the Kalimba, the wheel covers the box’s sound holes instead.

  • Ring, damp or dead stroke

    Let released keys ring, lay a hand across the bar, or keep the mallet pressed into it from the stroke. Stopping the transport damps everything by hand.

  • Seven tunings

    Equal temperament, Bala 7-equal, Chopi timbila, 5-equal pentatonic and Sambla, plus two measured keyboards: a Birifor gyil and a Sena valimba.

  • Velocity that reaches into the model

    Velocity changes the physical contact as well as the level. Choose Soft, Linear, Hard or Sigmoid response, then let velocity drive eleven controls, from Hardness to Artifacts. New devices and the factory patches start with Linear.

  • Three captures, three rooms

    Blend independent Close, Room and Piezo levels. Choose Wood, Chamber or Hall for the room capture, with subtle motion in the diffuse tail, and reverse the pickup polarity with Invert Piezo to explore its interaction with the microphones.

  • A finished main mix

    Raise Drive to add saturation to the Main mix; Width and Warmth shape its image and tone. Limit can add linked stereo compression with rear Attack and Release controls. Independent protection keeps peaks under control on every output.

In the rack

Built for Reason.

Seven audio outputs give you the main stereo mix, Close and Room stereo pairs, and a mono Piezo stem. The five direct outputs have their own calibration, including separate Kalimba trims, and stay independent of master Volume and the capture levels. All outputs share the same 2 ms delay.

Note and Gate CV play the instrument, and five more CV inputs modulate Hardness, Position, Coupling, Sympathetic and Volume.

The rear panel holds Tune, Detune and Scale, 8 to 24 voices, Quality from native 1× to 8× and the limiter’s timing. Front-panel sound controls are automatable and Remote-mappable.

Rack Extension version history

Maremba release history.

Version 1.5.7 is the current Rack Extension in the Reason Shop. Maremba was first released there as 1.5.0 on 13 October 2026. Buy or try Maremba in the Reason Shop.

1.6.5d1 Unreleased · Development build · 10 November 2026

Lower CPU in the Close capture, the Room and the sympathetic resonance, with the same sound. Quality starts at 1× again.

1.6.1d1 Unreleased · Development build · 6 November 2026

About an eighth less CPU on the development machine, with the same sound.

1.6.0d2 Unreleased · Development build · 3 November 2026

The Close capture uses a synthesized head and ear response. Room and Close processing reuse calculations for lower DSP cost, and eight Close-heavy factory patches are rebalanced for the new capture.

Reason Shop publication is separate from this development build.

1.5.7 Current Reason Shop version

The 20 factory sounds are refined with gentler velocity depths set for each patch and selected resonance adjustments. Load a factory patch afresh to hear them; saved patches keep their own settings.

A Walnut & Champagne panel with a handwritten title and a wider, clearer header. Quality adds native 1× processing and now reads 1×, 2×, 4×, 8× in ascending order, keeping saved settings. Factory balance and Main headroom are revised, the bar, resonator and Kalimba pickup balance refined, and repeated notes release more reliably in Reason, including Damp note-offs during a Quality change. Sound and level can differ from older versions even when saved controls keep their values.

1.5.0 13 October 2026 · First Reason Shop release

Softer wooden attacks, a more natural Decay at the control’s midpoint and lighter diffuse rooms with softened early reflections. Factory capture levels are rebalanced, and Main aligns the pickup with the airborne capture for a more consistent blend.

Drive now colours the complete Main mix, while the direct outputs stay clean.
